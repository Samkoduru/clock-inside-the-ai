The Clock Inside the AI

We fed days of the week to an AI and recorded what happened inside it. Each glowing dot is one weekday, as the model sees it. Drag the scene to rotate.

Scroll ↓  ·  Everything shown is measured from a real model running on a laptop.

1 · The ring

The week is already a circle inside the model.

Each dot is a weekday as the model sees it, found by reading the model's internal numbers while it processed phrases like "Today is Monday". Monday to Sunday line up in order around a ring, like the numbers on a clock face.

That order is there from the very first layers: it is baked into the model's word embeddings. Shuffling the day labels almost never produces a ring this good, even after allowing the best of 28 layers (p = –).

Ring score (chart): 1 means the angles follow the true weekday order perfectly.

2 · Same clock, different language

Hindi's weekdays swing onto the same ring in the middle layers.

Orange dots are the Hindi names (सोमवार, मंगलवार…). Drag the slider through the model's layers. In the first layers the Hindi names are in their own arrangement. By layer 14–16 each one sits at the same angle as its English twin.

3 · A surprise

Tamil weekdays don't form their own ring.

Magenta dots are the Tamil names. They show no clean circle in this model (p = – after correcting for layer choice).

One untested explanation: all seven Tamil names end in the same word (கிழமை, "day"), so the final word piece carries little day information. We haven't checked, so treat it as a hypothesis.

4 · Months too

The twelve months make a bigger ring.

January to December form an almost perfect circle (ring score –) that builds up over the first seven layers or so, and the Hindi month names (जनवरी, फ़रवरी…) sit on it too.

5 · Is it just a trick of the maths?

We tested with random words.

Any 7 points can look a bit circular if you pick the best layer. So we ran 30 sets of 7 random objects (table, river, ladder…) through the same test. They average –; the best reached –. The weekdays scored –, and, more tellingly, almost no shuffled labelling of the weekdays does as well. Real, but the margin over the best random set is modest, so the shuffle test is the stronger evidence.

7 · How much of the clock do you need?

It takes three "gears."

A clock ring needs several interlocking cycles. Using just the basic circle (1 harmonic), moving the dial gets the model's answer right 26–31% of the time. With 2, about 60%. With all 3 it reaches 90% (layers 10–14). A random direction of the same size: 0%.

What we don't know

Honest limits

  • One model (Sarvam-1, 2B), one tokenizer, and a few hundred prompts. Not yet repeated on other models.
  • Layers were chosen after looking; the shuffle statistics correct for that, the dial results do not.
  • The dial moves the answer, but "ring vs straight line" differs only modestly (0.69 vs 0.63 of probability on the right neighbours). The scrambled control collapses to 0.27.
  • Tamil: no ring. We offer a possible reason, not a tested one.
  • Names like Tuesday may span several word pieces; we use the last piece.
